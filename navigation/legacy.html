Legacy

The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 set a precedent of restricting immigration based on race, going against the United States' promises of equality and liberty for all. This legacy of the Exclusion Act continued to shape future immigration policies such as the Scott Act of 1888, the Immigration Act of 1924, and the Geary Act of 1892, which all imposed further limitations on immigrants. The Scott Act prohibited Chinese immigrants from re-entering the country if they left. The Geary Act extended the Chinese Exclusion Act by requiring Chinese residents to carry a certificate of residence or risk deportation. The Immigration Act of 1924 continued the legacy of racialized restrictions on immigration, encompassing not just the Chinese but other groups such as Eastern Europeans and Japanese. In 1943, the Chinese Exclusion Act was repealed, but its effects continued to impact Chinese Americans for decades afterward. Many faced discrimination in hiring, daily life, and attempts to assimilate. The negative portrayals of Chinese immigrants in that period led to continued racism, discrimination, and stereotypes well into the 1900s. Even in recent years, Chinese Americans faced hate crimes during the COVID-19 pandemic, fueled by the same racial stereotypes and xenophobia shown in the past. In 2012, Congress finally passed a formal apology for the Chinese Exclusion Act for its injustice done to the Chinese and its lingering negative impacts.

"Chinese exclusion helped re-define the very ways in which Americans saw and defined race in relation to other immigrant groups and transformed America's relationship to immigration in general."

-Erika Lee(2002)
